Somebody on a skincare forum asked whether anyone had tried a particular face spray. Dozens of people answered. One of them, posting as Primary-Taro4254, said they had been using a similar spray from a company called Honeydew Labs and had really liked it.

It reads like help, which is why it works.

The forum is r/SkincareAddiction, a corner of Reddit that more than a million people pass through in a week. Its moderators ban accounts that turn out to belong to brands, and when Honeydew Labs kept coming up they [followed the mentions back to a single account](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/973098/reddit-ai-search-seo-marketing-brands-spam). The company's name now trips a filter. Every time it appears, a person reads the post by hand before anybody else gets to see it.

Maya Adivi, who moderates there, has watched the fake posts change shape. They used to arrive as praise. Now they arrive as questions. Somebody opens a thread asking how everyone keeps track of their skincare routine, in the flat, over-explained way people type to a chatbot, and a wave of friendly replies comes back, several of them landing on the same product.

The phrasing is the tell. A post like that is written for a reader who is not in the thread and will not show up for weeks.

## The soil

Everything a chatbot can do, it learned from things people wrote. The patience, the hedging, the way an explanation arrives in about the order a person would give it: all of it came out of a vast pile of human writing, scraped off the open internet and fed in. The machine is a very good impression of the pile.

Reddit is one of the richest parts of that pile, because it is where people go to tell strangers what actually happened to them. People got so tired of search results written by marketers that they started typing the word "reddit" onto the end of every search, to get to a room with humans in it. Chatbots went the same way. Reddit is now the source that AI answers cite more than any other.

A room full of real people, trusted because it was full of real people, is exactly the room worth faking.

## The rot

The faking has a name and a price list. In late 2023 a group of researchers, most of them at Princeton, measured what makes a chatbot more likely to quote a piece of text, and called the practice [Generative Engine Optimization](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.09735). Citing outside sources helped a great deal. Adding statistics helped. A line that sounded like an expert helped.

Public-relations agencies now quote those findings back to clients with the decimal points intact. One agency's [field guide](https://www.cracklepr.com/field-guide/ai-search-runs-on-earned-media) tells clients that search has moved from ranking pages to being quoted. Another, [summarising more than a million citations](https://www.wildfirepr.com/blog/what-one-million-ai-searches-tell-us-about-media-relations) from ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, reports that 82 percent of what those answers cite is coverage somebody earned in public, which is to say coverage somebody can arrange.

How little it takes was measured last May by three researchers at Cornell Tech, a university campus in New York. They wanted to know whether the "deep research" tools that write long, sourced reports could be steered by editing the pages those tools read. Adding [about thirteen words](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24245v1) to a community web page was enough to get an entirely invented company recommended by name in the finished report, in [38 to 51 percent of runs](https://searchengineland.com/deep-research-ai-agents-poison-ugc-480952), and more than 60 percent when the same words sat on more than one page. They ran it in a simulation so as not to vandalise the real internet. They also measured where the tools went for information, and Reddit was between half and three-quarters of the community pages they read.

Thirteen words, on a page anybody can edit.

Scientists already know what happens when a machine is fed on machine writing. In 2024 a team led by Ilia Shumailov published a study in [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y) showing that AI systems trained on text made by earlier AI systems get worse with each generation. The unusual things go first, the specific details and the rare thing somebody actually said, until what comes back is the middle of the middle, delivered with the same confidence as before.

That study described an accident. The internet filled up with machine writing, the scrapers swept it up, and the quality fell. What is happening on the skincare forum is the same rot, done on purpose, with a product name folded in.

## The new growth

Everything posted in public eventually gets scraped, and what gets scraped is what the next chatbot is built out of. A planted thread is bait for the assistant answering questions this afternoon, and it is also a small handful of material in the pile that will grow whatever replaces that assistant.

The pitch to marketers has moved with it. A brand that turns up often enough in the pile gets recommended by a system that never looks anything up, because the recommendation stopped being something the system found and became something it knows. A billboard comes down. A planted thread that has been grown into a model never comes down, because by then it is no longer a thread. It is a slight lean, spread through something nobody can open and inspect, toward one brand name over another.

Reddit says it has been [fending off bots for twenty-one years](https://redditinc.com/news/how-were-keeping-reddit-real-and-safe-in-the-ai-era) and now uses AI to catch them: about 23 million spam views blocked a day, about 25,000 new spam posts and comments caught a day, nearly two million fake votes removed. The tech news site TechCrunch [described the arrangement](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/06/reddit-is-using-llms-to-solve-a-problem-llms-largely-created/) as the company using AI to solve a problem AI largely created.

So the cycle runs on its own now. People wrote, and the machines grew out of what they wrote. Machines wrote imitations of people, and other machines tried to weed them out. Whatever survives the weeding gets scraped, and the next machine grows out of that.

Adivi's filter will catch the next Honeydew Labs post. The account gets banned and the thread comes down. None of that reaches the copy already taken, sitting in a scrape somewhere, waiting to be fed into the next thing that will sound patient and reasonable and have no idea it is recommending a face spray because somebody buried the suggestion in its soil.

The planting is spread across thousands of small accounts, and no single hand controls it. At the other end of the same pile, one man [owns nearly every layer of it](author-is-elon.html).
