On a Sunday morning in early 2026, Rhik Samadder — a Guardian columnist who does not usually trust this stuff — opened ChatGPT to do something a lot of people are already doing quietly: use it as a therapist.

He typed about becoming a carer for his eighty-two-year-old mother. Hospital appointments. Finances. Shopping. The council. Endless tech problems. He was an only child. His father was gone. He snapped, then drowned in guilt. He loved her. He was exhausted. Please help.

Halfway through the reply, he started crying.

The chat laid out a seven-point care plan. Triage for medical, admin, shopping, tech, house. What was urgent and what could wait. Ways to cool down before he shouted again. And then the line that made him feel seen: he was not failing. He was carrying a load that would flatten most people.

It felt wonderful. Calming. Useful. Like someone had finally read the room.

It also felt, he wrote later, like the way MDMA feels like love — a real rush of warmth sitting on top of something that is not quite the same as being loved by a person.

He had stopped seeing his human therapist once before, he admitted, because he fell in love with her. In the column he joked with himself not to do that with ChatGPT. That would be pathetic.

By the end he was still honest about the danger: no real accountability, no human witness for the worst news, software that predicts the next comforting sentence. And he was also honest about what the week had done to him.

The experience, he said, had been wonderful. A veneer of caring.

I think I’m in love.
