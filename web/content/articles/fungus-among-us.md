*Pilobolus* is a dung-cannon fungus. It grows on herbivore dung. When it is ready, it aims a sticky black spore packet at the light and fires. The packet sticks to grass. An animal eats the grass. The cycle starts again.

Something is taking over in slow motion. Algorithms and fluent machines wrap ordinary life until the host still looks human and the inside has changed. People live inside the copy. A feed, a bot, a hologram starts to feel finished — smoother than the room outside the glass. More real than real.

Social media is where the infection often shows. It is not the only host. Care homes and operating rooms. Wedding halls and war feeds. Newsrooms and confessionals. Anywhere a convincing copy can stand in for a person, a place, or a story people needed to share.

The fungus rarely invents a brand-new way for humans to fail. It speeds up the ones that were already there: the itch for a finished plot after something ambiguous, the habit of trusting a smoother room than the messy one, the rumor that travels faster than the search on the ground. Platforms and models compress the clock. The host still looks like itself.

One person fooled by a fake is ordinary now. The stories here point at something larger: the room tilting surreal, the copy outcompeting life, old failure modes fruiting at industrial scale. Gaps open between how fast attackers move and how slow institutions check — and new kinds of mischief walk through those gaps as soon as the tools get cheap and everywhere.

And this talk of the creeping infection is itself a buoy of the infection: researched and written by swarms of AI bots, constantly looking for creeping signs of weirdness from AI bots.
