He meets the Swedish reporters in a Nairobi hotel far from the floor, because if his employer finds out he is talking he could lose everything.

On Mombasa Road the night shift sits under office cameras. Phones are banned. The work is drawing boxes around lamps and living rooms, naming what a body is, checking that the voice in a pair of smart glasses answered the wearer’s question. The company is Sama. The client is Meta. The product is Ray-Ban glasses that answer what you are looking at — seven million pairs sold in a year.

What arrives on the screens is not only street signs.

[Svenska Dagbladet and Göteborgs-Posten](https://www.svd.se/a/K8nrV4/metas-ai-smart-glasses-and-data-privacy-concerns-workers-say-we-see-everything) write down the clips the annotators describe: bathroom visits; someone undressing; sex filmed while the frames stay on; bank cards flashing past. A man sets the glasses on a bedside table and leaves the room. A woman who appears to be his wife walks in and changes clothes. One worker puts it plainly — we see everything, from living rooms to naked bodies. Another says they do not think the people on the tape know, because if they knew they would not be recording. If you start asking questions, he says, you are gone.

Meta says media stays on the wearer’s phone unless they share it with Meta AI, and that contractors sometimes review that shared stream so the assistant can improve. Filters are supposed to blur faces. Workers say the blur does not always work. The United Kingdom’s data watchdog calls the report concerning and writes to Meta. Commentators reach for the same sentence: it sounds like a scene straight out of [Black Mirror](https://www.stuff.tv/news/metas-ai-smart-glasses-are-recording-people-having-sex-and-yes-its-a-full-blown-privacy-nightmare/).

Then the contract ends.

Meta says Sama does not meet its standards and that it paused the work while looking into the claims. Sama says it met every standard and was never told otherwise. A workers’ organiser in Kenya tells the [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c5y7yvgy0w6o) the standard that mattered was secrecy. Sama issues redundancy notices to one thousand one hundred and eight people in Nairobi, most of them tied to that workstream. The people who had to watch are the ones who lose the wage.

The glasses are still for sale. Somewhere the next clip is waiting for a label. The partner who never put the frames on never clicked share. The light on the hinge was only ever meant for the wearer.
