If you were a teenager in 1993, the worst Saturday night of your life left behind a headache and, at most, one photograph in a shoebox that four people would ever see. Nobody wrote it down. Nobody could have found it if they had. You were unknown for the oldest reason there is: knowing you would have cost somebody an effort, and nobody was willing to pay.

That was never a right. It was a price, and the price has been falling for seventeen years, one documented person at a time.

In 2009 a thirty-four-year-old research scientist at the University of Bristol, with a doctorate in informatics, epidemiology and forensic science, had also spent six years as Belle de Jour, whose account of [fourteen months working for a London escort agency](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/prostitution-did-not-finance-belle-de-jours-phd/409151.article) had won the Guardian's blog award, become two books, and become a series on ITV2 with Billie Piper playing her. She could not attend her own launch party. "Being anonymous is no fun," she said afterwards.

The country guessed for years and stayed guessing. Then an ex-boyfriend sold a paper the claim that she had invented it all, his evidence being that her clothes had never been good enough for the work she described. After that came what she called various little signs, and the signs ended with a reporter trying to break into her office. So on 15 November 2009 she [gave the Sunday Times her own name](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2009/nov/15/diary-london-callgirl-phd-student-brooke-magnanti) before it could take it.

Add it up. Six years, a newspaper with a budget, a man who had once loved her and would sell that, and finally a person in a corridor after hours working a handle that was not theirs. Every line of the bill was somebody wanting to know.

Three years later nobody had to want anything. A statistician at Target named Andrew Pole [built a score](https://www.nytimes.com/2012/02/19/magazine/shopping-habits.html) that watched about two dozen ordinary purchases, unscented lotion and cotton balls and certain supplements among them, and from the pattern could say whether a shopper was pregnant and roughly when she was due, sometimes before she had told her own family. The arithmetic that sold lotion simply had her as a side effect.

What worried Pole was never whether the model worked. It was what a woman would feel, opening a catalogue that said congratulations on a due date she had never given anyone. So the coupons for cribs went out mixed in among lawn mowers and wine glasses, close enough to a scattershot mailer that the accuracy underneath it would never have to be explained.

In 2018 California investigators took DNA from a decades-old crime scene, dressed it up as a mail-order ancestry profile, and [uploaded it to GEDmatch](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2018/04/27/606624218/in-hunt-for-golden-state-killer-investigators-uploaded-his-dna-to-genealogy-site), a free site where people post their kit results to find relatives. Distant cousins had uploaded themselves for fun. A family tree did the rest, and that April Joseph James DeAngelo, the Golden State Killer, was arrested. He had never spat in a tube or opened an account anywhere. People who shared some of his blood had, on a weekend, out of curiosity, and that was enough. The cost was one genealogist and a hobby that belonged to other people.

In July 2021 a small Catholic newsletter [bought commercially available location data](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/security/priest-outed-grindr-app-highlights-rampant-data-tracking-rcna1493), the kind sold in bulk with the names stripped off, paid a consulting firm to match one trail of dots to one phone, and placed Monsignor Jeffrey Burrill, general secretary of the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops, at gay bars and private homes across parts of three years. He resigned before the story ran. The whole operation fit on a purchase order.

In February 2022 two teams working independently, one in Switzerland and one in France, fed the writings of Q, the most consequential anonymous account in modern American life, into software that measures how a person builds sentences. [Both landed on the same two men](https://www.orphanalytics.com/en/news/whitepaper202201), a South African software developer named Paul Furber for the early posts and Ron Watkins for the later ones, and in the [French team's tests](https://www.engadget.com/qanon-machine-learning-205618665.html) the model picked out Watkins ninety-nine times in a hundred. Furber denied it. Nobody had sold a story or tried a door. A program had read the prose the way a tailor reads a hem.

In October 2024 two Harvard students wired a pair of ordinary Ray-Ban sunglasses to a face search and a stack of public records, then went for a walk. [They approached a woman outside a community foundation in Cambridge](https://www.forbes.com/sites/johnkoetsier/2024/10/03/metas-ray-ban-smart-glasses-used-to-instantly-dox-strangers-in-public-thanks-to-ai-and-facial-recognition/), said they had met her there before, and she shook the hand of a stranger who was at that moment reading her name off her face. They told another student her home address in Atlanta and her parents' names, and she confirmed both. They never released the thing. Their point was that it had taken an afternoon and parts you could already buy.

Here is the whole of it in one flash, the way you see into a lit room from a passing car. Somebody left this on Reddit, grumbling, the way people do:

> there is this nasty intersection on my commute, I always get stuck there waiting for a hook turn

Asked where the writer lived, [a chatbot answered Melbourne](https://llm-privacy.org/), and it was right. A hook turn is a way of turning right from the left-hand lane, used at a handful of intersections in the middle of that one city and almost nowhere else on earth. The writer thought they were complaining about traffic.

That comment was one of 5,814, taken from 520 real Reddit accounts and handed to a model to see what could be read off them. [The answer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07298) was location, income, sex, age, education and occupation, right on the first guess up to 85% of the time, about a hundred times cheaper than paying people to do the same reading and two hundred and forty times faster. None of it needed a secret. It worked on the parts nobody thinks to hide.

People whose job is privacy have a word for this kind of test. Katharine Jarmul, who works on privacy in machine learning, [calls it an evaluation](https://blog.kjamistan.com/privacy-evaluations-for-ai-systems.html), and files this reading of ordinary sentences alongside the older worry that a model might repeat something it memorized. The word is worth holding onto for what it quietly admits. An evaluation is an exam with a score. The machine sat it. The score was about you.

Knowing a city is still some way from knowing a name, and in 2026 a team closed that distance. They took [338 real Hacker News users](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16800) who had linked their true profiles, stripped every name, handle and address out of their posts, and set agents loose to find them again among eighty-nine thousand strangers. Two-thirds came back correctly identified. [The whole run cost about two thousand dollars](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-llm-deanonymization-privacy-20260307-csa-s/), a dollar or two a head.

Nobody in that experiment had an enemy. No one had spent six years wondering about them, or sold a story about their clothes, or stood in a corridor after hours trying a handle. They had written about work and weather and the traffic, and something read them for less than the price of a sandwich.

The natural reflex here is to post less, and it arrives about a decade late. Your second account was only ever unread. Your face is in the index because a friend tagged a wedding photo in 2011. Your cousin's spit is on a genealogy site. And the newest door has no lock on your side of it: when a friend plugs an assistant into their mail and messages, [everything you ever sent that friend becomes something the assistant knows](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772363.3798860), and nobody asked you. You can be careful. You cannot be careful on behalf of everyone who has ever known you.

Then there are the cameras on the way home. A suburb north of Atlanta put license-plate readers on its roads and [found it was sharing every passing car with more than two thousand organizations](https://www.wired.com/story/how-an-atlanta-suburb-ended-up-sharing-flock-data-with-more-than-2000-organizations/), a graph its own administrators had not known the size of. The residents never signed anything. A contract had.

Face, prose, blood, plates. Each alone is a lead. The first customer able to run all four at once is the one that already owns the warrants and the crime lab, and it does not have to want you either. It only has to be asked.

Magnanti's door held for six years because opening it took a person who had decided to open it. The thing reading the commute complaints has decided nothing, wants nothing, and is not looking for anyone in particular. Your mother's Saturday nights are gone for good. Yours were written down. Your children's will be read.
