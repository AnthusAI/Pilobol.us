On a cobbled street in Strasbourg in mid-July 1518, a woman named Frau Troffea began to dance.

Chronicles put the day around the fourteenth. She danced through the heat until she collapsed, then rose and danced again. Her husband begged her to stop. By the next day her feet were swollen and bloody. She kept moving.

By the end of the week, roughly thirty people had joined her. Within a month the crowd was near four hundred — women and men hopping through the market, the alleys, the streets, day and night. Some fell from exhaustion, heart failure, or hunger. One contemporary line says many danced themselves to death.

The city fathers tried the cure that made sense to them: more dancing. They opened guild halls. They hired musicians with drums and pipes. They paid people to dance with the afflicted, hoping the bodies would tire and the fit would break. The music did not empty the street.

When that failed, they loaded dancers onto carts and sent them to the shrine of St. Vitus outside the city. Accounts describe small crosses and red shoes pressed on the travelers as a saint’s remedy for a saint’s curse — the belief that St. Vitus could seize a limb and would not let go until the shrine settled the debt.

Six chronicles from the time keep the outline. Names diverge. Starting dates slip. The measures the magistrates ordered are written down in more than one hand: the paid pipers, the carts, the shrine. What the pages agree on is the scene itself — a shared story about a curse that moved from one body to the next until an entire city had to answer it with music and then with wheels.

The summer ended. The carts rolled. The cobbles that held Frau Troffea’s first steps had already held hundreds more.
