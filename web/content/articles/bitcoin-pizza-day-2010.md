On 18 May 2010, a Florida programmer named Laszlo Hanyecz posted a simple offer on an early bitcoin message board.

I’ll pay 10,000 bitcoins for a couple of pizzas.. like maybe 2 large ones so I have some left over for the next day. Standard toppings — onions, peppers, sausage, mushrooms, tomatoes, pepperoni. No weird fish topping or anything like that. You could bake them yourself and bring them over, or order delivery. He just wanted food to arrive without cooking it.

He lived in Jacksonville, zip 32224. Someone on the thread noted that 10,000 coins could sell for about forty-one dollars that day. Another wondered how a Domino’s in the United States would take payment from Europe. Laszlo said Papa John’s already took cards by phone and online.

Three days later the board was still quiet. So nobody wants to buy me pizza? Is the bitcoin amount I’m offering too low? He answered his own itch in the next post: I just think it would be interesting if I could say that I paid for a pizza in bitcoins.

On 22 May he reported success. I just want to report that I successfully traded 10,000 bitcoins for pizza. Pictures. Thanks jercos.

Another early user — the board name jercos — had paid for two Papa John’s pizzas and got them delivered to Laszlo’s house in Florida. The coins moved on the ledger. The boxes arrived at the door. Somebody on the thread called it a great milestone. Laszlo kept the offer open for a while, noting the two pizzas cost about twenty-five dollars, maybe thirty with a tip, and that his one-year-old smeared leftover slices across her face.

Years later, on *60 Minutes*, he put the day in one line: It made it real for some people, I mean it certainly did for me.

The first known buy of ordinary goods with bitcoin did not need a bank window or a government seal on the table. It needed a forum post, a second person willing to treat the numbers as money, and a pizza chain that still took dollars for cheese. The ledger held dinner because enough people already acted as if it could.
