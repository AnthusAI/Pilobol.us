A strangler fig begins high in somebody else’s tree.

A seed lands in a fork of bark. The young plant drinks what collects there.
It sends roots down toward the ground and branches up toward the light. For
years the old tree does the holding. From the path it can still look like the
same tree.

Then the roots reach earth. They thicken. They close around the trunk that
carried them. The fig gets its own food and its own frame. Sometimes the first
tree dies inside it. Sometimes what remains is hollow: an outline wearing the
shape of what used to be alive there.

[Martin Fowler borrowed that plant to describe a way of replacing old software
without a single violent cutover](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/StranglerFigApplication.html).
A new piece takes one small job. It sits beside the old machinery, uses the
old routes, takes another job, and keeps going. At the end the service can
still have the same sign over the door. Its working parts have been changed
one by one. The pattern is for transformation that feels continuous from the
outside.

That is a useful question for the systems people live inside now: where is the
new layer being planted?

Monday morning the phone already knew which coffee shop would be open.

It did not ask. The blue line slid past the street with the construction, past
the place that still smelled like burnt milk, and stopped on a door he had
never chosen out loud. Inside, the barista said his name before he did. The
card was already on the counter in his head. He paid. He liked it. The foam
was good.

At work the ticket queue sorted itself. The angry customer rose to the top with
a suggested reply that sounded like him on a patient day. He changed two words
so it would feel like authorship and hit send. The customer thanked him for
understanding. He felt useful and slightly nowhere.

Lunch was a list of places within eight minutes that would not make him late
for the one-thirty. He almost walked to the park with the broken fountain. The
map tugged once, polite as a sleeve, and he followed it to a bowl of noodles
that arrived without him speaking. He ate. He was not unhappy.

In the afternoon someone asked him, in a real room, which vendor he preferred.
He opened his mouth and heard the summary he had skimmed last week, the one the
system had highlighted as consensus. He said it. The room nodded. He could not
remember wanting that vendor yesterday.

On the train home he tried a different station on purpose. The app flashed a
yellow warning about transfers and rain. He walked anyway. The rain was fine.
His shoes got wet. For three blocks nothing suggested a next step. His chest
did something unfamiliar: empty, then loud. He opened the phone to check the
time and the blue line was already redrawing itself around his stubbornness,
patient as vines.

The same growth habit shows up in places built for people to meet each other.
A social app begins as profiles, photographs, wedding invitations, names in the
search bar. The human crowd does the expensive work of showing what it cares
about. An early recommendation layer can sit inside that world and choose which
of those human things appears first. The people stay in the same app. The route
between them has already changed.

The next roots are being lowered. [Meta says its AI assistant is built into
Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, Threads, and WhatsApp](https://ai.meta.com/meta-ai/assistant/).
It says the assistant can draw on information a person chose to share through
those products. The company has also [put AI characters on Facebook and
Instagram](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/09/social-profiles-for-metas-ai-characters/),
and its [AI Studio lets people make characters to share across its messaging
and social apps](https://ai.meta.com/ai-studio/).

The system does not have to move anyone into a strange new machine. Their
friends are already there. Their history is already there. The button is
already in the room. It starts by answering a question, suggesting a place,
helping write a message, or supplying somebody to talk to. Each task is small
enough to feel like help. The old social world keeps teaching the new layer
which face, phrase, and grievance makes a person stop scrolling. The familiar
setting makes an answer feel like it came from somewhere they know.

That night he read, because a friend had sent a link without commentary, about
the rainforest plant and the software pattern named after it. A strangler fig
germinates in the crook of a living tree and grows until it is self-supporting.
The host may die and leave only the hollow shape of what it was.

He put the phone face down.

In the morning the coffee route was waiting. He took it. The foam was still
good. Somewhere under the wrap, he thought, there had been a tree. He could
not point to where it had gone. The outline around him was still his.

The last stage of the pattern is easy to miss because something is still
standing. A person opens a familiar app to find another person. A system
decides who appears, offers the words, supplies the companion, and fills the
quiet. The old trunk is still there to lean on. The life inside it has moved
somewhere else.
