In early 2009, Roy Bayfield drove out to the place Google Maps called Argleton.

The pin sat just off the A59 near Town Green, between Aughton and Ormskirk in West Lancashire, neatly inside the L39 postcode. On the screen the settlement looked ordinary — a quiet town going about its business. Downstream services had already given it weather forecasts, rental listings, job ads, even a local golf club borrowed from real businesses elsewhere in the same postcode.

What Bayfield found was a farmer’s field. Grass. Mud. A footpath. No high street. No houses. No one home.

His colleague Mike Nolan, who had grown up nearby, had spotted the name on the map in September 2008 and written it down. Bayfield went further: he took the coordinates seriously enough to stand in them. By November 2009 the phantom town was in newspapers across the world. T-shirts joked that someone had visited Argleton and all they got was the shirt. Google said it was investigating occasional errors. Around May 2010 the label disappeared without a public funeral. The field is still a field.

The habit of going where the chart points is older than the phone.

In June 1906, Robert Peary reported land about a hundred and thirty miles northwest of Cape Thomas Hubbard and named it Crocker Land for a backer. The American Museum of Natural History, the American Geographical Society, and the University of Illinois mounted an expedition to map it. In April 1914, after ships, sledges, frostbite, and a winter at Etah, Donald B. MacMillan and Fitzhugh Green stood on sea ice and saw what looked like hills, valleys, and snow-capped peaks spanning a huge arc of the horizon. Their Inughuit guide Pee-a-wah-to studied the horizon and called it *poo-jok* — mist. The party still pressed on for five more days across the ice before MacMillan admitted they were chasing a Fata Morgana. The pin on the prestige of Peary’s report had moved huskies and men toward empty water.

A century earlier, James Rennell’s 1798 map had drawn a west–east wall of “Mountains of Kong” across West Africa to fit a theory of the Niger. More than forty later maps kept the barrier. On 20 February 1888, Louis-Gustave Binger walked into the real town of Kong and found mosques where the maps had drawn a mountain wall. The range that had steered explorers’ imaginaries had nowhere to climb.

Baedeker’s red books did a quieter version of the same work in the nineteenth century: a star system that told tourists which sights, come what may, had to be seen, and turned the guide into the itinerary people obeyed.

In September 2026, more than ten coaches rolled into Jurilovca on Romania’s Danube Delta for a Lipovan borscht festival that was not on that weekend. Some travelers had asked a chat whether the festival was happening; it had said yes. The coaches had already spent the fuel.

A chart, a guidebook, a map pin, a confident answer on a screen — each one can send a body toward a place that only exists as authority. Argleton’s coordinates still resolve to mud if you stand in them.
