On Mombasa Road in Nairobi the annotation floor runs in shifts. Phones are not allowed in. Cameras watch the room. The work itself is patient and repetitive: draw a box around the lamp, name what the object is, check that the assistant answered the wearer's question correctly, move on to the next clip.

The clips come from Ray-Ban Meta glasses, worn by people on the other side of the world who asked the assistant about something in front of them. Meta says footage stays on the wearer's phone until they share it with Meta AI, that contractors then review some of what was shared so the assistant can improve, and that the material is filtered before anyone sees it. The workers say the blurring does not always work.

What comes through is not only street signs and restaurant menus. Reporters at Svenska Dagbladet and Göteborgs-Posten [wrote down what the annotators described watching](https://www.svd.se/a/K8nrV4/metas-ai-smart-glasses-and-data-privacy-concerns-workers-say-we-see-everything): people on the toilet, people undressing, people having sex with the frames still on, bank cards turned over in front of the lens. In one clip a man sets the glasses down on a bedside table and leaves, and a woman walks into the room and changes her clothes. "We see everything," one worker said, "from living rooms to naked bodies."

That is the job, and somebody has to do it, because the assistant cannot yet tell a bathroom from a kitchen without being shown which is which a few hundred thousand times. The floor on Mombasa Road was the part of the product that could look at a room and know what it was, installed ahead of the software and holding the seeing open until the machine could manage it alone.

Two things were being collected there, and only one of them was boxes around lamps. Every time a worker marked an answer right or wrong, they handed over a piece of judgment — what a useful reply looks like, where the line sits, when a thing is simply wrong. The boxes taught the assistant what a lamp is. The corrections taught it how to be the person checking.

That arrangement is not unusual and it is not going away. A job gets done and it pays, and the record of how it was done is the actual product, worth more to whoever keeps it than the person who made it is.

They were handled to match. Phones stay outside the building. Cameras run on the workers while the workers watch the footage. One of them told the reporters that anybody who started asking questions about where the stream came from would be gone. When they finally did speak, they met the journalists in a hotel well away from the floor, and not one of them allowed their name to be printed, because being identified would have cost them the work.

The story ran in February. In March the United Kingdom's data watchdog called the account concerning and [wrote to Meta](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c0q33nvj0qpo) about its duties. On 16 April, Sama told 1,108 people in Nairobi that their jobs were redundant and [gave them six days](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c5y7yvgy0w6o). Meta said Sama had not met its standards, and did not say which standards, or when it had decided. Sama said nobody had ever told it that it fell short. Naftali Wambalo of the Africa Tech Workers Movement said the standard being enforced was secrecy.

The glasses are still for sale. The labelling goes on somewhere else now, done by somebody else, and the assistant keeps getting better at knowing what a room is.

A model can be shown a bedroom ten thousand times and it will never mention it to anybody. That was the one part of the job the people on Mombasa Road could not do. Everything else about them turned out to be replaceable inside six days, and was.
