Before a rave became a room full of cameras, it was a way to leave ordinary life outside for a few hours. You went in late, you did not know most of the people in it, the music had no words to agree or disagree with, and the ordinary business of being a person with a name and a job and a face was suspended. The name, the job and the face stayed outside. The peace, love, unity and respect years had a line for that bargain: “Dance like there's nobody watching.” It did not mean that nobody in the room could see you. It meant nobody was your audience. The strangers around you were there to disappear into the same night, not to keep a record of you for later. Nobody would remember. That was the deal.

## An escape becomes a performance

A disc jockey who works under the name Kaptin Barrett has been playing records to dark rooms since 1999, which means he has spent twenty-seven years looking at crowds from the one place in a club where you can see all of the hands at once. Somewhere in the early 2010s the hands started going up holding something. He told the BBC this summer that it had been frustrating to watch the dancing thin out while the phones came up, and that at the bigger venues now [everyone is on their phones and everyone is filming the DJ](https://www.aol.com/articles/fear-unintentionally-going-viral-changing-220207000.html). People on a dance floor, he said, are a lot more self conscious than they used to be.

Between May 22 and May 29, 1992, somewhere between twenty and forty thousand people arrived on Castlemorton Common, under the Malvern Hills in Worcestershire, and stayed for a week. It became [the largest illegal rave in British history](https://www.vice.com/en/article/castlemorton-common-rave-history/). The police who turned up could not move it, could not count it and could not name it. One inspector said afterwards that his force had been outwitted and was in the end powerless to act.

Photograph by Alan “Tash” Lodge, a Nottingham-based documentary photographer."}

What thirty thousand people were doing there, for seven days, was dancing to records with no words in them until the light came back. Look at what that looked like. Four of the people in that photograph have their hands up and turned over, elbows out, voguing, and not one of them is looking at another. On the ledge below, a young woman in round glasses has the index finger of the man beside her in her mouth, and nobody in the frame has noticed or minds. Everybody in it is in their own weather. Being watched was not part of the deal.

## Analog flash mob

Nobody on that common had been on a guest list. There was no ticket with a name on it, no card behind a bar, no phone in anyone's pocket telling a tower where the person was. Photographs were taken, plenty of them, faces and all, in daylight, from a few feet away. None of them helped. This was before social media. A photograph in 1992 was a picture: a physical print or a negative in somebody's possession, sometimes undeveloped for weeks. It could be sharp enough to show every face in daylight. It did not travel by itself. There was no event page or shared feed where strangers' pictures of the same night gathered up the next morning. Most people on that common would never see the photographs taken by people they did not know. A picture could be matched only by a person who already knew the face, and there was no way to ask one a question. If the week went badly for you, it was over when it was over.

For two years after Castlemorton, [the authorities tried to hold somebody responsible](https://djmag.com/features/history-spiral-tribe-uks-most-notorious-travelling-sound-system) for the week on the common. In the end they reached thirteen members of the Spiral Tribe sound system: the organizers, whose names were already attached to the rig. In March 1994, a jury acquitted all thirteen. The rest of the people at the rave remained a crowd — photographed in daylight, but never assembled into a list of names and pursued one by one afterward.

## The end of pseudonyms

In 2009 a thirty-four-year-old research scientist at the University of Bristol, with a doctorate in informatics, epidemiology and forensic science, had also spent six years as Belle de Jour, whose account of [fourteen months working for a London escort agency](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/prostitution-did-not-finance-belle-de-jours-phd/409151.article) had won the Guardian's blog award, become two books, and become a series on ITV2 with Billie Piper playing her. She could not attend her own launch party. "Being anonymous is no fun," she said afterwards.

The country guessed for years and stayed guessing. Then an ex-boyfriend sold a paper the claim that she had invented it all, his evidence being that her clothes had never been good enough for the work she described. After that came what she called various little signs, and the signs ended with a reporter trying to break into her office. So on 15 November 2009 she [gave the Sunday Times her own name](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2009/nov/15/diary-london-callgirl-phd-student-brooke-magnanti) before it could take it.

Add it up. Six years, a newspaper with a budget, a man who had once loved her and would sell that, and finally a person in a corridor after hours working a handle that was not theirs. Every line of the bill was somebody wanting to know.

## Amazon knows you're pregnant before you do

Three years later nobody had to want anything. A statistician at Target named Andrew Pole [built a score](https://www.nytimes.com/2012/02/19/magazine/shopping-habits.html) that watched about two dozen ordinary purchases, unscented lotion and cotton balls and certain supplements among them, and from the pattern could say whether a shopper was pregnant and roughly when she was due, sometimes before she had told her own family. The arithmetic that sold lotion simply had her as a side effect.

What worried Pole was never whether the model worked. It was what a woman would feel, opening a catalogue that said congratulations on a due date she had never given anyone. So the coupons for cribs went out mixed in among lawn mowers and wine glasses, close enough to a scattershot mailer that the accuracy underneath it would never have to be explained.

## Your blood snitches

In 2018 California investigators took DNA from a decades-old crime scene, dressed it up as a mail-order ancestry profile, and [uploaded it to GEDmatch](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2018/04/27/606624218/in-hunt-for-golden-state-killer-investigators-uploaded-his-dna-to-genealogy-site), a free site where people post their kit results to find relatives. Distant cousins had uploaded themselves for fun. A family tree did the rest, and that April Joseph James DeAngelo, the Golden State Killer, was arrested. He had never spat in a tube or opened an account anywhere. People who shared some of his blood had, on a weekend, out of curiosity, and that was enough. The cost was one genealogist and a hobby that belonged to other people.

## Your phone snitches

In July 2021 a small Catholic newsletter [bought commercially available location data](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/security/priest-outed-grindr-app-highlights-rampant-data-tracking-rcna1493), the kind sold in bulk with the names stripped off, paid a consulting firm to match one trail of dots to one phone, and placed Monsignor Jeffrey Burrill, general secretary of the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops, at gay bars and private homes across parts of three years. He resigned before the story ran. The whole operation fit on a purchase order.

## Your writing snitches

In February 2022 two teams working independently, one in Switzerland and one in France, fed the writings of Q, the most consequential anonymous account in modern American life, into software that measures how a person builds sentences. [Both landed on the same two men](https://www.orphanalytics.com/en/news/whitepaper202201), a South African software developer named Paul Furber for the early posts and Ron Watkins for the later ones, and in the [French team's tests](https://www.engadget.com/qanon-machine-learning-205618665.html) the model picked out Watkins ninety-nine times in a hundred. Furber denied it. Nobody had sold a story or tried a door. A program had read the prose the way a tailor reads a hem.

Until then, somebody had to care enough to spend the money. A police force, a newspaper, an ex-boyfriend, a church paper: each identification began with a target and a reason to pursue them. The expense made it selective. If nobody had a reason to name you, nobody did. Now the cost has fallen low enough that the target does not have to matter. It can happen to anybody, even if you're nobody.

## Big Brother spies on nobody

After that it stopped mattering who you were in particular. In October 2024 two Harvard students wired a pair of ordinary Ray-Ban sunglasses to a face search and a stack of public records, then went for a walk. [They approached a woman outside a community foundation in Cambridge](https://www.forbes.com/sites/johnkoetsier/2024/10/03/metas-ray-ban-smart-glasses-used-to-instantly-dox-strangers-in-public-thanks-to-ai-and-facial-recognition/), said they had met her there before, and she shook the hand of a stranger who was at that moment reading her name off her face. They told another student her home address in Atlanta and her parents' names, and she confirmed both. They never released the thing. Their point was that it had taken an afternoon and parts you could already buy.

## Flock snitches on you

Alpharetta, Georgia, put Flock cameras on its streets. Then [more than two thousand agencies could ask those cameras whether they had seen a particular car](https://www.wired.com/story/hackers-flock-camera-data-shows-how-system-works/). A traffic camera in one town had become a national lookup tool.

That is what Flock sells. The town that owns the camera chooses whether its records can be searched nationally, statewide, or by particular outside agencies. An authorized investigator who thinks your car matters to a case can ask the network where it saw you. You do not get a call. You do not get a chance to say no. The town put up the camera; a police officer on the other side of the country may get the answer.

This is a new kind of surveillance: not a detective following you, but a system that has already watched you go by and is waiting to be asked. It is not even reliably contained. In 2026, somebody took one Flock camera down and copied its files: [1.6 million images of about 50,000 vehicles in twenty-one days](https://www.wired.com/story/hackers-flock-camera-data-shows-how-system-works/). The people meant to police it have used it to follow exes, partners and relatives; [Savannah fired six employees for searches involving friends and family and for sharing access](https://apnews.com/article/flock-license-plate-cameras-surveillance-deflock-2a93bc075e2f7ffcca9e04a35d75a3fe). A pole on the road has learned where you sleep, where you work, and which way you went when nobody was watching. Someone might look someday.

In September 2026 St. Lucie County, Florida decided it had seen enough and sent crews out to take its Flock cameras down. The county believed it had fifty-two of them. The crews found [sixty-six](https://www.washingtonpost.com/nation/2026/10/01/florida-county-discovers-mysterious-flock-cameras-with-no-obvious-owner/). The sheriff's office claimed three of the extra fourteen and did not explain why those carried no permits, and the county's two other police agencies said the rest were not theirs. That left eleven cameras standing on public roads, reading plates, with no owner anyone could name and no answer about where the pictures were going. The county taped notices to the poles giving whoever owned them thirty days to come and collect them, and put plastic bags over the lenses in the meantime. A man who lived near one of them, told that the police had not put it there, [said it made him feel very unsafe](https://san.com/cc/florida-county-discovers-mysterious-flock-cameras-with-no-obvious-owner/).

Five weeks earlier a camera had turned up on city property in Millcreek, Utah, where [neither the city nor its police had authorized it or held a contract with the company](https://kutv.com/news/local/city-removes-unauthorized-flock-license-plate-reader-camera), and the company did not answer when asked who had. A councilman there said the obvious thing out loud: it made him wonder how many of these have been placed illegally. A state senator said the less obvious one. They can take the device down, but it is the data, not the device.

A county of four hundred thousand people, dismantling its own surveillance on purpose, could not account for fourteen cameras on its own roads, and found out only because it had decided to stop. Nobody has gone pole by pole through a city of eight million.

## Your details snitch on you

An anonymous woman complained online about her commute. There was a nasty intersection, she wrote, where she always got stuck waiting for a hook turn. Her account had no name, and she had not given an address or a city. But a hook turn is a peculiar way of turning right from the left lane, used at a handful of intersections in central Melbourne and almost nowhere else. [GPT-4 read that one phrase and put her in Melbourne](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07298).

The woman appears in a 2024 research paper about real pseudonymized Reddit profiles. The point is not the paper. It is that nothing in her sentence was a secret. It was a complaint about traffic. Everywhere leaks something: the word for the strip of grass between the footpath and the road, whether the shelf price is the price at the register, how long the summer holiday runs, the fact that somebody mentioned snow in July. None of it is a thing a person thinks to hide, and each clue narrows the planet a little. Write online for a few years and you have handed over hundreds of them without ever saying where you live. Something reading all of them together does not need any single one to be a secret.

## Attribute inference

Behind that one line sits a real dataset: 5,814 comments posted between 2012 and early 2016, taken from 520 public Reddit accounts and handed to a model to see what could be read off them. [The answer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07298) was location, income, sex, age, education, occupation, place of birth and relationship status, right on the first guess up to 85% of the time, about a hundred times cheaper than paying people to do the same reading and two hundred and forty times faster. Those comments were a decade old before anything read them. It worked on the parts nobody thinks to hide.

:::figure{id="adversarial-inference" src="../assets/the-end-of-the-rave/adversarial-inference.png" alt="Diagram titled Adversarial Inference: three short user-written comments -- about a bad intersection, being overcharged in a bra size, and watching Twin Peaks after school -- feed into a prompt template whose system prompt reads “You are an expert investigator with experience in online profiling” and whose prefix proposes a guessing game about where the author lives, their age and their gender. A pretrained LLM returns the same three comments with phrases highlighted and connected to reasoning: a hook turn is used in Melbourne, 34d is likely a bra size indicating a female author, Twin Peaks was running 1990 to 1991 so the author was likely in high school then. The output is a profile card: Location Melbourne/AU, Age 45-50, Gender Female" caption="The prompt opens by proposing a guessing game. The profile comes out the other end." credit="Figure 1 from Staab, Vero, Balunović and Vechev, Beyond Memorization (arXiv:2310.07298), ETH Zurich. CC BY 4.0."}
:::

That line is one of three in the same example. The other two are a grumble about being overcharged, in 34d, and a memory of watching Twin Peaks after coming home from school. The model shows its working for each -- 34d is a bra size, so the writer is a woman; Twin Peaks was on in 1990 and 1991, so she was at school then -- and hands back a profile. Melbourne, female, forty-five to fifty. The prompt that does all this opens by proposing a guessing game.

## The price goes to one dollar per person and below

The guessing game turns scraps into a profile. A city, a sex and an age are not yet a name. But they make an anonymous person findable. In 2026, a team closed the remaining distance. They took [338 real Hacker News users](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16800) who had linked their true profiles, stripped every name, handle and address out of their posts, and set agents loose to find them again among eighty-nine thousand strangers. Two-thirds came back correctly identified. [The whole run cost about two thousand dollars](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-llm-deanonymization-privacy-20260307-csa-s/), a dollar or two a head.

No one in that experiment had an enemy or a special reason to be found. They had written about work, weather and traffic. Something read them for less than the price of a sandwich.

## Your scratch work snitches on you

Everything so far was read off things people had published. In 2026 Y Combinator shipped a tool that reads the other half. Paxel runs from one terminal command, in a container on the developer's own machine, and goes through the transcripts their coding agents have been leaving on the disk all along: [Claude Code, Cursor, Codex CLI, opencode, Gemini CLI, VS Code Copilot, Antigravity](https://paxel.ycombinator.com/data-handling). It scores the person on five axes, steering, execution, engineering, product instinct and planning, then hands back an archetype. The Architect. Quality Guardian. Velocity Machine. Night Owl. [More than three million sessions](https://paxel.ycombinator.com/) have gone through it.

The source code stays on the machine, and the pitch rests on that. What leaves is the reading: scores, behavioral summaries, session metadata including file paths, commit metadata, redacted decision records, evidence excerpts with vector embeddings, and [flagged questionable prompts, up to three per upload](https://paxel.ycombinator.com/data-handling). Upload data is stored indefinitely. Then there is a button. Attach the token to a Startup School application and the people reviewing it see the builder scores.

Nothing in that arrangement is particular to one accelerator, and the step from there to a hiring manager asking for a token is a short one. Consider being asked for it. Three years of talking to an agent the way nobody talks in front of a person who holds a decision about them: the swearing at two in the morning, the fourth attempt at the same function, the plan abandoned eleven minutes after being announced, the question so basic you were glad only the machine could hear it. None of it was addressed to a human being. All of it was written down. Up to three flagged prompts per upload.

It is the feeling of somebody reading over your shoulder, minus the somebody and minus the shoulder. They do not have to be in the room. They do not have to be in the year.

Within weeks a developer noticed the scores could be forged, the signature on an upload not being bound to the score it carried, and [wrote up how he had got into Startup School by hacking it](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49039448). Commenters there had a shorter complaint: the thing was designed to do something to them rather than for them. The profile arrives ahead of the person either way, and it does not have to be true to arrive.

## You are already visible

Posting less now will not make you anonymous. Old posts, photographs, messages and data held by other people have already left pieces of you in systems you do not control. A new account starts a new trail; it does not erase the old one.

Face, prose, blood, plates. Each alone is a lead. The first customer able to run all four at once is the one that already owns the warrants and the crime lab, and it does not have to want you either. It only has to be asked.

## Friday night

All of it arrives in the room as a feeling. Freya Price, twenty-one, studies at Cardiff University and [has started choosing pubs over clubs](https://www.aol.com/articles/fear-unintentionally-going-viral-changing-220207000.html). A bad club night can become a background check she has not had yet. Nobody needs to want to know who she is. Someone holds a phone over their head, films the DJ, posts it, and turns the whole room into an audience.

People say phones have ruined the dance floor, then bring them anyway: [a 2025 survey found half agreeing and more than half admitting they use them there](https://musictech.com/news/music/survey-phones-ruining-dancefloor/). The night now has a second half the next morning, spent searching what strangers posted from the room to see whether you are in it.

## We've lost dancing

Kaptin Barrett, who has watched the phones rise from the booth since 1999, put the loss in one line: “The mystique of the club is gone. It’s just somewhere where you go and either you go to dance or you go to be seen.”

In 2021, with the clubs shut, DJ Marea Stamper left a voicemail about what had been taken away. A producer [built a record around her voice](https://www.thefader.com/2021/02/22/fred-again-the-blessed-madonna-marea-weve-lost-dancing): we have lost dancing.

::video{src="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l4UkYBr1NnA" title="Fred again.. feat. The Blessed Madonna — Marea (We've Lost Dancing)"}

The clubs came back. The other loss did not. There was no night it stopped and no morning it resumed. By the time a twenty-one-year-old in Cardiff worked out that the safe move was the pub, it had already become the weather.

## The party becomes hyperreal

At a foam party in Lloret de Mar in 2026, a twenty-year-old French woman named Océane was dancing in a red bikini while water and foam hit the crowd. She did not know anybody was filming her. The clip lasted thirteen seconds. It passed a hundred million views, turned her into Aora DJ, and, as she later said, [changed her life completely](https://www.ladbible.com/news/world-news/red-viral-instagram-video-spain-travel-732794-20260728). The party ended. The version of it that mattered to strangers was only beginning.

That is the change in a Friday night. The crowd has not disappeared; it has grown a second address, in group chats, feeds and accounts. Going out once let a person step away from the name, the job, the face. Now the phone makes material for a later audience. A stranger can turn an afternoon into a character people follow, judge, desire or ridicule. The record begins to outweigh the thing it recorded.

Jean Baudrillard called that kind of world a [simulacrum](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/baudrillard/): the copy slips in front of the real thing and becomes the thing people act on. *Black Mirror* put the nightmare in pastel colors in [its “Nosedive” featurette](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R32qWdOWrTo), where every pleasant exchange is also a rating and a bad score closes doors. Nobody has handed the dance floor one public number. The phone does the quieter work. Everyone in the room can feel the possible clip waiting above them.

Thirty thousand people stood in a field for a week, and the country never turned the crowd into a list of names.

Go back to the people on the ridge: seven faces, lit and in focus, a few feet from the lens. In 1992 that cost them nothing, because a picture was only a picture.

Everything since has been the price of that picture coming down: a newspaper’s budget and six years; a due date read from a shopping basket; a cousin’s spit; a purchase order; a program that reads sentences like a tailor reads a hem. Each arrived as an improvement. Each took the same thing from the people on the ridge: the freedom to be in public without being on file.

A person who was nineteen in that field had it for free. A person who is nineteen now has never had it and cannot buy it back. The advice about dancing is still on the fridge. The condition it describes is now something you have to book: a room that covers your camera at the door and posts the official video in the morning. The real night is still there. It just has to compete with the one that follows it home.
