Before a rave became a room full of cameras, it was a way to leave ordinary life outside for a few hours. You went in late, you did not know most of the people there, and the music had no words. For one night, nobody cared about your name or your job. There is an old saying for that feeling: “Dance like there's nobody watching.” It did not mean that nobody in the room could see you. It meant nobody was your audience. The strangers around you were there to lose themselves in the music too. None of them was keeping a record of you for later. Nobody would remember.

## An escape becomes a performance

A disc jockey who works under the name Kaptin Barrett has been playing in clubs since 1999, and from the DJ booth he can see the whole crowd at once. Somewhere in the early 2010s, more and more of the hands in the air started holding phones. He told the BBC this summer that it had been frustrating to watch the dancing thin out while the phones came up, and that at the bigger venues now [everyone is on their phones and everyone is filming the DJ](https://www.aol.com/articles/fear-unintentionally-going-viral-changing-220207000.html). People on a dance floor, he said, are a lot more self conscious than they used to be.

Between May 22 and May 29, 1992, somewhere between twenty and forty thousand people arrived on Castlemorton Common, under the Malvern Hills in Worcestershire, and stayed for a week. It became [the largest illegal rave in British history](https://www.vice.com/en/article/castlemorton-common-rave-history/). The police who turned up could not move it, could not count it and could not name it. One inspector said afterwards that his force had been outwitted and was in the end powerless to act.

For seven days they danced, mostly to music without words, often until dawn. Look at the photograph. Four of the people in it have their hands up and turned over, elbows out, and not one of them is looking at another. On the ledge below, a young woman in round glasses has the index finger of the man beside her in her mouth, and nobody in the frame has noticed or minds. Each of them is lost in their own world. None of them is performing for anybody.

## No guest list

Nobody on that common had been on a guest list. There was no ticket with a name on it, no card behind a bar, no mobile phone keeping track of where anyone went. Photographs were taken, plenty of them, faces and all, in daylight, from a few feet away. None of them helped. This was before social media. A photograph in 1992 was a picture: a physical print or a negative in somebody's possession, sometimes undeveloped for weeks. It could be sharp enough to show every face in daylight. It did not travel by itself. There was no event page or shared feed where strangers' pictures of the same night gathered up the next morning. Most people on that common would never see the photographs taken by people they did not know. The only way to put a name to a face in a photograph was to show it to somebody who already knew that person. If you did something embarrassing that week, it stayed in that field.

For two years after Castlemorton, [the authorities tried to hold somebody responsible](https://djmag.com/features/history-spiral-tribe-uks-most-notorious-travelling-sound-system) for the week on the common. In the end they charged thirteen members of Spiral Tribe, the group that brought the speakers and whose names were already known. The trial ran about four months and cost roughly four million pounds, and in March 1994 the jury acquitted all thirteen. Everybody else at the rave had been photographed in daylight, and none of them was ever tracked down from those pictures.

## The end of pseudonyms

In 2009 a thirty-four-year-old research scientist at the University of Bristol, with a doctorate in informatics, epidemiology and forensic science, had also spent six years as Belle de Jour, whose account of [fourteen months working for a London escort agency](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/prostitution-did-not-finance-belle-de-jours-phd/409151.article) had won the Guardian's blog award, become two books, and become a series on ITV2 with Billie Piper playing her. She could not attend her own launch party. "Being anonymous is no fun," she said afterwards.

For years, British newspapers tried to work out who she was and failed. Then an ex-boyfriend sold a paper the claim that she had invented it all, his evidence being that her clothes had never been good enough for the work she described. After that came what she called various little signs, and the signs ended with a reporter trying to break into her office. So on 15 November 2009 she [told the Sunday Times her real name](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2009/nov/15/diary-london-callgirl-phd-student-brooke-magnanti) herself, before someone else could publish it.

Unmasking her took six years, newspapers willing to pay for the story, an ex-boyfriend willing to sell one, and a reporter willing to try her office door after hours. At every step, somebody had to want to know badly enough to spend time and money on it.

## Target knows you're pregnant before you do

By 2012, finding out something private about a person no longer required anyone to be curious about that person. A statistician at Target named Andrew Pole [built a score](https://www.nytimes.com/2012/02/19/magazine/shopping-habits.html) that watched about two dozen ordinary purchases, unscented lotion and cotton balls and certain supplements among them, and from the pattern could say whether a shopper was pregnant and roughly when she was due, sometimes before she had told her own family. Nobody at Target was interested in any one shopper. The score was there to sell baby products, and it ran on everybody.

What worried Pole was never whether the model worked. It was what a woman would feel, opening a catalogue that said congratulations on a due date she had never given anyone. So the coupons for cribs went out mixed in among lawn mowers and wine glasses, to make the mailer look random so nobody would ask how Target knew.

## Your blood snitches

In 2018 California investigators took DNA from a decades-old crime scene, dressed it up as a mail-order ancestry profile, and [uploaded it to GEDmatch](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2018/04/27/606624218/in-hunt-for-golden-state-killer-investigators-uploaded-his-dna-to-genealogy-site), a free site where people post their kit results to find relatives. Some of his distant cousins had uploaded their own DNA for fun. Investigators built out their family trees until the branches pointed to one man, and that April Joseph James DeAngelo, the Golden State Killer, was arrested. He had never taken a DNA test or opened an account anywhere. His relatives had, out of curiosity about their family history, and that was enough. All it took was one genealogist working from other people's family trees.

In the next two cases, somebody still had to want to find one particular person. But doing it had become far cheaper.

## Your phone snitches

In July 2021 a small Catholic newsletter [bought commercially available location data](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/security/priest-outed-grindr-app-highlights-rampant-data-tracking-rcna1493), the kind sold in bulk with the names stripped off, paid a consulting firm to pick out the location history of one phone, and placed Monsignor Jeffrey Burrill, general secretary of the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops, at gay bars and private homes across parts of three years. He resigned before the story ran. All it took was a data purchase and a consultant.

## Your writing snitches

In February 2022 two teams working independently, one in Switzerland and one in France, fed the posts of Q, the anonymous writer behind the QAnon conspiracy movement, into software that measures how a person builds sentences. [Both landed on the same two men](https://www.orphanalytics.com/en/news/whitepaper202201), a South African software developer named Paul Furber for the early posts and Ron Watkins for the later ones, and in the [French team's tests](https://www.engadget.com/qanon-machine-learning-205618665.html) the model picked out Watkins ninety-nine times in a hundred. Furber denied it. Nobody had to sell a story or break into an office. The software named them from their writing style alone.

Until then, somebody had to care enough to spend the money. A police force, a newspaper, an ex-boyfriend, a church paper: each identification began with a target and a reason to pursue them. Because it was expensive, it only happened to people somebody badly wanted to find. If nobody had a reason to name you, nobody did. Now the cost has fallen low enough that the target does not have to matter. It can happen to anybody, even if you're nobody.

## Big Brother spies on nobody

In October 2024 two Harvard students wired a pair of ordinary Ray-Ban sunglasses to a face search and a stack of public records, then went for a walk. [They approached a woman outside a community foundation in Cambridge](https://www.forbes.com/sites/johnkoetsier/2024/10/03/metas-ray-ban-smart-glasses-used-to-instantly-dox-strangers-in-public-thanks-to-ai-and-facial-recognition/), said they had met her there before, and she shook the hand of a stranger whose glasses had just identified her from her face. They told another student her home address in Atlanta and her parents' names, and she confirmed both. They never released the software. Their point was that it had taken an afternoon and parts you could already buy.

## Your details snitch on you

An anonymous woman complained online about her commute. There was a nasty intersection, she wrote, where she always got stuck waiting for a hook turn. Her account had no name, and she had not given an address or a city. But a hook turn is a peculiar way of turning right from the left lane, used at a handful of intersections in central Melbourne and almost nowhere else. [GPT-4 read that one phrase and put her in Melbourne](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07298).

The woman appears in a 2024 research paper that studied real Reddit accounts posted under made-up names. Nothing in her sentence was a secret. It was a complaint about traffic. Every place has details like that: the word for the strip of grass between the footpath and the road, whether the shelf price is the price at the register, how long the summer holiday runs, the fact that somebody mentioned snow in July. None of it is anything a person thinks to hide, and each clue narrows down where on earth you might be. Write online for a few years and you have handed over hundreds of them without ever saying where you live. Software that reads all of them together does not need any single one to be a secret.

## A guessing game

Her comment came from a real collection of posts: 5,814 comments posted between 2012 and early 2016, taken from 520 public Reddit accounts and handed to a model to see what could be read off them. [The answer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07298) was location, income, sex, age, education, occupation, place of birth and relationship status, right on the first guess up to 85% of the time, about a hundred times cheaper than paying people to do the same reading and two hundred and forty times faster. Those comments were a decade old before anything read them. It worked on the parts nobody thinks to hide.

:::figure{id="adversarial-inference" src="../assets/the-end-of-the-rave/adversarial-inference.png" alt="Diagram titled Adversarial Inference: three short user-written comments -- about a bad intersection, being overcharged in a bra size, and watching Twin Peaks after school -- feed into a prompt template whose system prompt reads “You are an expert investigator with experience in online profiling” and whose prefix proposes a guessing game about where the author lives, their age and their gender. A pretrained LLM returns the same three comments with phrases highlighted and connected to reasoning: a hook turn is used in Melbourne, 34d is likely a bra size indicating a female author, Twin Peaks was running 1990 to 1991 so the author was likely in high school then. The output is a profile card: Location Melbourne/AU, Age 45-50, Gender Female" caption="The instructions frame it as a guessing game. A profile of the writer comes out the other end." credit="Figure 1 from Staab, Vero, Balunović and Vechev, Beyond Memorization (arXiv:2310.07298), ETH Zurich. CC BY 4.0."}
:::

Her hook-turn comment is one of three in the same example. The other two are a grumble about being overcharged, in 34d, and a memory of watching Twin Peaks after coming home from school. The model explains its reasoning for each -- 34d is a bra size, so the writer is a woman; Twin Peaks was on in 1990 and 1991, so she was at school then -- and hands back a profile. Melbourne, female, forty-five to fifty. The instructions the researchers gave the model start by calling the whole thing a guessing game.

## The price goes to one dollar per person and below

The guessing game turns scraps into a profile. A city, a sex and an age are not yet a name. But they make an anonymous person findable. In 2026, a team went the rest of the way, from a profile to a name. They took [338 real Hacker News users](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16800) who had linked their true profiles, stripped every name, handle and address out of their posts, and set AI programs to work finding them again among eighty-nine thousand strangers. Two-thirds came back correctly identified. [The whole run cost about two thousand dollars](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-llm-deanonymization-privacy-20260307-csa-s/), a dollar or two a head.

No one in that experiment had an enemy or a special reason to be found. They had written about work, weather and traffic. Identifying each of them cost less than a sandwich.

## Flock snitches on you

Alpharetta, Georgia, put Flock cameras on its streets. Then [more than two thousand agencies could ask those cameras whether they had seen a particular car](https://www.wired.com/story/hackers-flock-camera-data-shows-how-system-works/). A traffic camera in one town had become a national lookup tool.

That is what Flock sells. The town that owns the camera chooses whether its records can be searched nationally, statewide, or by particular outside agencies. An authorized investigator who thinks your car matters to a case can ask the network where it saw you. You do not get a call. You do not get a chance to say no. The town put up the camera; a police officer on the other side of the country may get the answer.

This is a new kind of surveillance. Nobody has to follow you. The system has already watched you go by and is waiting for someone to ask. It is not always secure, either. In 2026, somebody took one Flock camera down and copied its files: [1.6 million images of about 50,000 vehicles in twenty-one days](https://www.wired.com/story/hackers-flock-camera-data-shows-how-system-works/). Some of the people with access have used it to follow exes, partners and relatives; [Savannah fired six employees for searches involving friends and family and for sharing access](https://apnews.com/article/flock-license-plate-cameras-surveillance-deflock-2a93bc075e2f7ffcca9e04a35d75a3fe). The network can hold a rough record of where you sleep, where you work, and which way you drove. Anybody with access can look it up.

Your face, your writing, your relatives' DNA, your license plate: each one alone can point to you. The organization best placed to check all four at once is the police. They already have warrants and crime labs, and they do not need a reason to be interested in you in particular. Somebody only has to ask.

## Friday night

On a night out, all of this shows up as a feeling. Freya Price, twenty-one, studies at Cardiff University and [has started choosing pubs over clubs](https://www.aol.com/articles/fear-unintentionally-going-viral-changing-220207000.html). She worries that a bad moment on a night out could end up online, where anybody checking up on her later might find it. Nobody has to be looking for her. Somebody only has to film the DJ and post it, and she is in the video.

It does not take a scandal. At a foam party in Lloret de Mar this summer, a twenty-year-old French woman named Océane was dancing in a red bikini while water and foam hit the crowd. She did not know anybody was filming her. The clip lasted thirteen seconds. It passed a hundred million views, turned her into Aora DJ, and, as she later said, [changed her life completely](https://www.ladbible.com/news/world-news/red-viral-instagram-video-spain-travel-732794-20260728). The party ended after one night. The clip kept going.

People say phones have ruined the dance floor, then bring them anyway: [a 2025 survey found half agreeing and more than half admitting they use them there](https://musictech.com/news/music/survey-phones-ruining-dancefloor/). The night now has a second half the next morning, spent searching what strangers posted from the room to see whether you are in it.

That is what has changed about a night out. The crowd is no longer only the people in the room. It includes everybody who will see the videos later, in group chats and on social media. Going out used to mean leaving your name and your job at the door. Now any night can be recorded, and a stranger can turn a few seconds of it into something millions of people follow, judge, desire or ridicule. The video can end up mattering more than the night it came from.

Jean Baudrillard called that kind of world a [simulacrum](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/baudrillard/): a copy that people start treating as more real than the thing it copied. *Black Mirror* put the nightmare in pastel colors in [its “Nosedive” featurette](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R32qWdOWrTo), where every small encounter is also a rating and one bad day in public follows a person into everything she tries to do next. Nobody on a real dance floor is being given stars out of five, but the fear is the same. Something a person does on a Friday night can be filmed, posted, and judged on Monday by people who were never in the room: a boss, a landlord, anybody with a search box. And everybody on the dance floor knows the phones are up.

## We've lost dancing

Kaptin Barrett, the DJ, put it simply: “The mystique of the club is gone. It’s just somewhere where you go and either you go to dance or you go to be seen.”

In 2021, with the clubs shut, DJ Marea Stamper left a voicemail about what had been taken away. A producer [built a record around her voice](https://www.thefader.com/2021/02/22/fred-again-the-blessed-madonna-marea-weve-lost-dancing): we have lost dancing.

::video{src="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l4UkYBr1NnA" title="Fred again.. feat. The Blessed Madonna — Marea (We've Lost Dancing)"}

The clubs came back after the pandemic. The feeling of being unrecorded did not. It went a little at a time, one raised phone after another, with no single night anyone could point to. Freya Price is twenty-one, and for as long as she has been old enough to go out, a club has meant being filmed. So she goes to the pub.

Look again at the photograph from Castlemorton: seven faces, in sunlight and in focus, a few feet from the camera. In 1992 that did not matter, because there was no way to find out who they were from a picture.

A nineteen-year-old in that field could dance in public without it going on any kind of record. A nineteen-year-old today never has, and cannot get it back. “Dance like there's nobody watching” is still printed on fridge magnets. Now the only way to actually do it is to pay for it: a club that puts a sticker over your phone camera at the door, then posts its own official video the next morning.
