On a Newark block on Halloween night in 1938, people wrapped wet towels over their faces and ran from a gas raid.

There was no gas. What filled the air was a radio play.

That night, Orson Welles and the Mercury Theatre on the Air turned H.G. Wells’s *The War of the Worlds* into a string of news bulletins on CBS. An orchestra at a hotel. An interruption about strange gas on Mars. A Princeton astronomer. A metal cylinder in a farmer’s field at Grovers Mill, New Jersey. Heat rays. Poison drifting toward Manhattan. The voices borrowed the sound of emergency interruptions listeners had practiced during the Munich crisis a month earlier. Plenty of people tuned in late, after the Chase & Sanborn comedy break on another network, and met the invasion before they met the fiction credit.

Phone lines jammed. The New York Times switchboard took hundreds of calls that night. In Trenton, police circuits clogged with people asking whether New Jersey was under attack. More than six hundred letters later reached the young Federal Communications Commission — some demanding punishment, some praising the hour, many narrating what a living room did when the dial sounded like the end of the world.

The next morning, Welles — twenty-three — stood at a Studio One press conference denying that anyone had meant to terrify the country. The papers already owned a louder story: a wave of mass hysteria from coast to coast. Families packed to be evacuated. Geologists from Princeton hunting a meteor that was never there. A front page that made the stampede feel nationwide.

How far that stampede really ran is still contested. A Hooper survey that night found only about two percent of radio sets tuned to CBS; most of the country stayed with the comedy-variety show. Later fact-checks rate a coast-to-coast mass hysteria as mostly false — wire roundups of anecdotes, hospital-shock rumors that did not hold when checked, newspapers competing with radio for authority after Munich. Some late dial-ins were frightened. Some thought Germany had come; others heard invaders from Mars. The phones still jammed. The towels still went on.

Eleven years later, in Quito, a similar fake-newscast package ended with a mob burning a radio building and people dead. In 1938 America, the cylinder in the New Jersey field stayed empty. The people who answered it as news had already spent the night.
