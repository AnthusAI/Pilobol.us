A strangler fig begins high in somebody else’s tree.

A seed lands in a fork of bark. The young plant lives on whatever water collects
there. It sends roots down toward the ground and branches up toward the light.
For years the old tree holds it up. From the path it can still look like the
same tree.

Then the roots reach earth. They thicken. They close around the trunk that
carried them. Now the fig can feed itself and stand on its own. Sometimes the
first tree dies inside it. Sometimes the old tree rots away and leaves a hollow
fig in the shape of the tree that used to be there.

[Martin Fowler borrowed that plant to describe a way of replacing old software
without a single violent cutover](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/StranglerFigApplication.html).
A new piece of software takes over one small job. It runs beside the old
system, takes requests through the same channels, then takes over another job,
and another. At the end the service can look the same to the people using it,
with the same name, but the parts that do the work have all been replaced, one
by one. The point is a change that users never notice happening all at once.

The same thing can be asked of the apps and services people rely on every day:
where is new software quietly taking over jobs people used to do themselves?

Monday morning, a man’s phone had already picked a coffee shop that would be
open.

It did not ask. The blue line on the map routed him past the street with the
construction, past the café that still smelled like burnt milk, and ended at a
door he had never actually chosen. Inside, the barista said his name before he
did. In his head he had already paid. He paid. He liked it. The foam was good.

At work, the software had already sorted his queue of customer complaints. The
angriest customer was at the top, with a suggested reply already written that
sounded like him on a patient day. He changed two words so it would feel like
he had written it, and hit send. The customer thanked him for understanding.
He felt useful, and also as if he had not really done anything.

For lunch the phone offered a list of places within eight minutes that would
not make him late for his one-thirty. He almost walked to the park with the
broken fountain. The map sent him one polite nudge, and he followed it to a
bowl of noodles that arrived without him having to say a word. He ate. He was
not unhappy.

In the afternoon someone asked him, in a real room, which vendor he preferred.
He opened his mouth and heard himself repeating a summary he had skimmed last
week, the one the software had marked as what most people agreed on. He said
it. The room nodded. He could not remember wanting that vendor yesterday.

On the train home he tried a different station on purpose. The app flashed a
yellow warning about transfers and rain. He walked anyway. The rain was fine.
His shoes got wet. For three blocks, nothing told him where to go next. He felt
something unfamiliar: first a kind of emptiness, then his heart pounding. He
opened the phone to check the time, and the blue line had already redrawn his
route to fit the way he had chosen, as patient as a vine.

The same slow takeover shows up in apps built for people to meet each other.
A social app begins as profiles, photographs, wedding invitations, names in the
search bar. The people using it do the hard work of showing what they care
about. Early on, the app starts using software to choose which of those posts
appears first. The people stay in the same app. What they see of each other is
already being chosen for them.

Now the next step is under way. [Meta says its AI assistant is built into
Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, Threads, and WhatsApp](https://ai.meta.com/meta-ai/assistant/).
It says the assistant can draw on information a person chose to share through
those products. The company has also [put AI characters on Facebook and
Instagram](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/09/social-profiles-for-metas-ai-characters/),
and its [AI Studio lets people make characters to share across its messaging
and social apps](https://ai.meta.com/ai-studio/).

Meta does not have to get anyone to move to a strange new app. Their friends
are already there. Their history is already there. The assistant’s button is
already on the screen they use every day. It starts by answering a question,
suggesting a place, helping write a message, or supplying somebody to talk to.
Each task is small enough to feel like help. Everything people do in the old
social app keeps showing the new AI which faces, phrases and complaints make a
person stop scrolling. The familiar setting makes an answer feel like it came
from somewhere they know.

That night he read, because a friend had sent a link without commentary, about
the rainforest plant and the software pattern named after it. A strangler fig
germinates in the crook of a living tree and grows until it is self-supporting.
The host may die and leave only the hollow shape of what it was.

He put the phone face down.

In the morning the phone had the coffee route ready. He took it. The foam was
still good. He thought about the tree inside the fig. He could not point to the
moment he had stopped choosing things for himself. From the outside, his life
still looked like his.

The last stage is easy to miss because everything still looks the same from
outside. A person opens a familiar app to find another person. Software decides
who appears, offers the words, supplies the AI companion, and fills the
silences. The app is still there, and it still looks like a place to find
people. But more and more of what happens inside it is being done by the
software.
