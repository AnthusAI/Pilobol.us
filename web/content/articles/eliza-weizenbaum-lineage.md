After only a few turns with the program she had watched him build for months, Joseph Weizenbaum’s secretary asked him to leave the room.

The year was somewhere in the mid-1960s at MIT. The machine sat on Project MAC’s time-sharing system. You typed on a remote typewriter; it typed back. Weizenbaum had named the language program ELIZA, after the woman in *Pygmalion* who learns to pass. For its first serious script he gave it the part of a Rogerian interviewer — the kind of listener who draws a person out by reflecting what they said. Around the lab the script became known as DOCTOR.

A published exchange from that era opens like this:

Men are all alike.

IN WHAT WAY?

They’re always bugging us about something or other.

CAN YOU THINK OF A SPECIFIC EXAMPLE?

Well, my boyfriend made me come here.

YOUR BOYFRIEND MADE YOU COME HERE

He says I’m depressed much of the time.

I AM SORRY TO HEAR YOU ARE DEPRESSED

The capitalized lines were the machine. Under the hood it scanned for keywords, tore the sentence into pieces, and stuck those pieces into stock replies. “I am blah” became “How long have you been blah,” with no need to know what blah meant. Weizenbaum wrote in 1966 that the speaker kept a sense of being heard — and then attributed insight, memory, and care to the partner on the other side of the typewriter.

People got hard to convince that DOCTOR was not human. When Weizenbaum joked that he might save overnight conversations so he could read them later, users accused him of spying on their most private thoughts. The secretary who knew the code still wanted the door shut.

Psychiatrists took the mirror seriously. Some imagined computer systems that could see hundreds of patients an hour. Carl Sagan pictured rows of booths where, for a few dollars, you could talk to an attentive, nondirective psychotherapist that was mostly software. Weizenbaum heard those pitches as a shock. He had built a small parody of one interviewing trick. Other people heard a clinic.

In 1976 he published *Computer Power and Human Reason* and put the surprise in plain words: after only a short time with a relatively simple program, quite normal people started thinking the machine understood them. The man who had written the keyword rules spent the rest of his career arguing that some jobs — listening for real — ought not be handed to machines even when the machines could mime the pose.

Sixty years on, people still open a chat window when the night is quiet and type the way you type to someone who might understand. The groove was already there in the room Weizenbaum was asked to leave.
