In 2009 the woman in an office at the University of Bristol, in a group that studied children's health, was thirty-four and held a doctorate from Sheffield in informatics, epidemiology and forensic science. She had also spent six years as Belle de Jour, whose account of [fourteen months working for a London escort agency](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/prostitution-did-not-finance-belle-de-jours-phd/409151.article) had taken the Guardian's blog award, become two books, and become a series on ITV2 with Billie Piper playing her. She had not been able to attend her own launch party. "Being anonymous is no fun," she said afterwards.

The country guessed for years and stayed guessing. Then an ex-boyfriend sold a paper the story that she had made the whole thing up, his evidence being that her clothes had never been good enough for the work she described. After that came what she later called various little signs that something might be going on, and the signs ended with a reporter trying to break into her office. So she took it off them, and on 15 November 2009 she [gave the Sunday Times her own name](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2009/nov/15/diary-london-callgirl-phd-student-brooke-magnanti).

Add it up: six years, a national newspaper with a budget, a man who had once loved her and was willing to sell that, and finally a person standing in a corridor working a handle that was not theirs.

Here is what the same job looks like now. Somebody left this on Reddit, grumbling, the way people do:

> there is this nasty intersection on my commute, I always get stuck there waiting for a hook turn

Asked where the writer lived, [a chatbot answered Melbourne](https://llm-privacy.org/). A hook turn is a way of turning right from the left-hand lane, used at a handful of intersections in the middle of Melbourne and almost nowhere else on earth. They thought they were complaining about traffic.

That comment was one of 5,814, taken from 520 real Reddit accounts and handed over to see what could be read off them. The answer was location, income, sex, age, education and occupation, right on the first guess up to 85% of the time, roughly a hundred times cheaper than paying people to do the same reading and two hundred and forty times faster. None of it needed a secret. It worked on the parts nobody thinks to hide.

Knowing somebody's city is still a long way from knowing their name. In 2026 a team closed that distance: they took [338 real Hacker News users](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16800) who had linked their true profiles, stripped every name, handle and address out of their posts, and set agents loose to find them again among eighty-nine thousand strangers. Two-thirds came back correctly identified. The whole run cost about two thousand dollars, which works out at a dollar or two a head.

Nobody in that experiment had an enemy. No one had spent six years wondering about them, or sold a story about their clothes, or stood in a corridor after hours trying a handle. They had written about work and weather and the traffic, and something read them for less than the price of a sandwich.

Magnanti's door held for six years because opening it took a person who had decided to open it. The thing going through the commute complaints has decided nothing, wants nothing, and is not looking for anyone in particular.
