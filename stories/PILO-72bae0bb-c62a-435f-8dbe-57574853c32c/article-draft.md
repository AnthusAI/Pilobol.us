Somebody on a skincare forum asked whether anyone had tried a particular hypochlorous acid spray. Dozens of people answered. One of them, posting as Primary-Taro4254, said they had been using a similar spray from Honeydew Labs and had really liked it.

It reads like help, which is why it works.

r/SkincareAddiction has more than a million people passing through it in a week. Its moderators ban accounts that turn out to belong to brands, and when Honeydew Labs kept surfacing they [followed the mentions back to a single account](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/973098/reddit-ai-search-seo-marketing-brands-spam). The brand's name now trips a filter. Every time it comes up, a person reads the post by hand before anyone else sees it.

People started adding the word reddit to their searches because they wanted a room with humans in it. That is what made a human worth faking.

Maya Adivi, who moderates there, has watched the spam change shape. It used to arrive as praise. Now it arrives as a question. Someone opens a thread asking how everyone keeps track of their skincare routine — the flat, over-explained phrasing of a person typing to a machine — and a wave of helpful replies comes back, several of them landing on the same product.

The phrasing is the tell. A post like that is written for somebody who is not in the thread and will not arrive for weeks. Reddit has become the source that AI answers cite more than any other, which makes a convincing Reddit thread the cheapest way to put a sentence in an assistant's mouth. Eventually someone asks a chatbot how to keep track of a skincare routine, and the answer comes back calm and organised and sourced, and what it is sourcing is the conversation that was put there for it to find.

It was not always a trade. In late 2023 a group of researchers, most of them at Princeton, published a paper that gave the practice its name and then measured it — [Generative Engine Optimization](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.09735), by Pranjal Aggarwal, Vishvak Murahari and colleagues, later presented at a data-mining conference. They ran text through answer engines and recorded what made a passage more likely to be quoted. Citing outside sources helped enormously. Adding statistics helped. Adding a line that looked like an expert saying something helped. It reads as research into how these systems choose, which is what it was.

The field guides quote the findings back with the decimal points intact. Crackle PR's [version](https://www.cracklepr.com/field-guide/ai-search-runs-on-earned-media) lists the levers — better than a hundred percent more visibility for citing sources, forty-odd percent for statistics, near thirty for a quotation that sounds like an authority — and tells clients that search has moved from ranking pages to being quoted, and that the communications desk now holds the only lever that moves what an assistant says about them. Wildfire, [summarising an analysis](https://www.wildfirepr.com/blog/what-one-million-ai-searches-tell-us-about-media-relations) of more than a million citations across ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, reports that 82 percent of what those answers cite is earned media.

Somebody measured how the machine decides what to believe, published it, and within two years the measurement was a service with a monthly fee. Stripped of the vocabulary, the work being sold is the placing of sentences where a model will find them.

How little that takes was measured last May by three researchers at Cornell Tech. Kaiwen Zhang, Isaac Triedman and Vitaly Shmatikov built a rig they called [WARP](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24245v1) to find out whether the deep-research tools that write long sourced reports could be steered by editing the pages they read. They could. Appending roughly thirteen words to a community page was enough to get an entirely invented company named in a finished report as a cited recommendation, in [38 to 51 percent of runs](https://searchengineland.com/deep-research-ai-agents-poison-ugc-480952), and above 60 percent when the same text sat on more than one page. They ran it in simulation rather than vandalising the live web. They also recorded which sources their systems leaned on hardest: community pages were about a fifth of everything retrieved, and Reddit was between half and three-quarters of that.

Thirteen words, appended to a page anybody can edit.

It is not only skincare. In June, 404 Media found sellers working r/biohackers the same way, seeding peptide recommendations into threads that read like people comparing notes on their own bodies.

All of that concerns what a model fetches while somebody waits for an answer. The longer play is what it keeps.

Everything posted in public is eventually crawled, and what is crawled is what the next system is built out of. A sentence planted on a forum this year is not only bait for the assistant answering questions this afternoon; it is a candidate for the pile that trains whatever replaces that assistant. The pitch being made to marketers has moved accordingly. A brand that turns up often enough across the crawl gets recommended by a system that never looks anything up, because the recommendation has stopped being something it found and become something it knows. Placement today, and permanence in whatever is built next.

That is a different kind of purchase. A billboard comes down. A planted thread that has been read into a model does not come down, because by then it is not a thread. It is a small adjustment to the odds that a machine says one brand name instead of another, distributed across something nobody can open and inspect.

The original promise of these systems was that they had read what people wrote. Everything they can do comes from that — the phrasing, the hedges, the way an explanation arrives in roughly the order a person would give it. The pile was human, and the machine was a very good impression of the pile.

What happens when the pile stops being human has been studied, and the finding is not comfortable. In 2024 Ilia Shumailov and colleagues published work in [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y) showing that systems trained on their own recursively generated text degrade across generations. The tails go first — the unusual, the specific, the rare thing somebody actually said — until what comes back is the middle of the middle, delivered with no less confidence than before. They were describing an accident of hygiene. The web filled up with machine writing, the crawlers ate it, and the quality fell.

The difference on r/SkincareAddiction is that somebody meant it. The thread was generated to be eaten. It was written by a system that learned to sound human, posted where the crawlers graze, to teach the next system that a particular face spray is what a helpful person recommends — so that it will say so on its own, unprompted, to somebody who will never know there was a thread.

Reddit says it has [been fending off bots for twenty-one years](https://redditinc.com/news/how-were-keeping-reddit-real-and-safe-in-the-ai-era) and now uses language models to find them: around 23 million spam views blocked a day, about 25,000 new spam posts and comments caught a day, close to two million fake votes stripped. TechCrunch [described the arrangement](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/06/reddit-is-using-llms-to-solve-a-problem-llms-largely-created/) as the company using language models against a problem language models largely created.

That is the shape of it now. Machines writing the posts, machines catching the posts, machines reading whatever survives the catching and learning to sound like it.

Adivi's filter will catch the next one. The account gets banned and the thread comes down, and none of that reaches the copy that was already taken — sitting in a crawl somewhere, being read into the next thing that will sound patient and reasonable and have no idea that it is quoting an advertisement for a face spray.
