At the entrance to the Gleiwitz radio station, on the night of 31 August 1939, there was a dead man in a Polish uniform.

His name was Franciszek Honiok. He was forty-three, a local farmer, unmarried. The SS had arrested him the day before. Someone dressed him for the part and left him where police and press would find him.

Inside the building that evening, a seven-man SS team had already finished the other half of the night’s work. Disguised as Polish insurgents, they pushed past the German staff at Sender Gleiwitz — the border transmitter whose wooden tower still stands today in what is now Gliwice, Poland — seized a microphone, and announced in Polish that the broadcasting station was in Polish hands.

Within hours German radio was carrying the story as news: Poles had stormed the studio; a body lay on the steps. The BBC picked up the German News Agency version — an attack around eight o’clock, a statement in Polish from the studio, German police opening fire, several of the Poles “reported killed,” numbers still unknown.

The following morning, 1 September, German forces invaded Poland.

Along that same border that night, similar scenes were staged at Hochlinden and at a forestry lodge at Pitschen — more men in Polish uniforms left dead as canned “proof.” At Gleiwitz the shared evidence was simpler and louder: a frequency that sounded like a raid, and one farmer arranged as the body that made the raid look real.

The wooden tower is still there. The war that answered the broadcast started at dawn.
