---
title: The face that isn’t dad
date: 'Tuesday, September 8, 2026'
description: Parents feed old family photos into chatbots, post the shiny versions
  on Facebook, and the album fills with lookalikes. Kids who never met those years
  inherit the copy.
standfirst: On Facebook, a restored family photo gets the likes. Side by side with
  the original, the faces are different people.
---

Your dad has discovered the chatbot. He uploads the faded prints — the ones with
scratches, the ones with almost no color — and asks it to restore them. Seconds
later a shiny modern picture comes back. He posts it. Old classmates leave hearts.
The feed loves nostalgia that looks new.

You scroll his timeline and stop.

That is not his face.

It is close enough to fool a stranger. Close enough that if you never lived those
years, you would not know to doubt it. In
[How-To Geek](https://www.howtogeek.com/ai-photo-restoration-is-erasing-your-familys-real-history/),
Justin Duino describes exactly this: parents gleefully running damaged family
photos through ChatGPT, then sharing the results on Facebook. When his dad posts
an AI-milled portrait of himself, the mismatch is obvious and disturbing. When
the photos are from before Justin was born, he cannot tell which face was real
without putting the pictures side by side. He never met his grandfathers in
person. Photographs were the only evidence of what they looked like. Now the
internet is full of people voluntarily replacing those faces with doppelgängers
in rooms that never existed.

The tool does not recover lost detail. It invents a new photograph using the old
one as a hint. A Library of Congress family portrait run through the same kind of
prompt comes back with extra furniture, a different poster on the wall, and
people who look hired to sit in someone else’s chairs.

On forums, the complaint arrives every week with a side-by-side:
[“Remini made my grandma look like a stranger.”](https://bestphoto.ai/blog/do-ai-restored-photos-change-too-much)
The models were trained on millions of sharp modern faces. Hand them a blurry
1960s portrait and they pull the features toward an average young adult — smoother
skin, symmetric features, ceramic pores. A vendor press kit for a “restore”
product ships dozens of examples where ages shift, makeup appears, and small
faces go grotesque
([PetaPixel on ON1](https://petapixel.com/2026/03/25/on1-restore-ai-turns-old-family-photos-into-grotesque-nightmare-fuel/)).

Somewhere a child will grow up with the shiny album. The likes stay. The master
print stays in a drawer, if it stays at all.

You keep the side-by-side open. One face you remember. One face the family is
learning to prefer.
