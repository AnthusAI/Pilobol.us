In 1945, a Fortune caption under a photograph of General Mills board chairman James Ford Bell called him the real Betty Crocker.

Readers who had written her for cake advice met that line after years of treating her as a neighbor in the kitchen. Fortune said she was known to ninety-one percent of housewives. In the same breath it noted that ten lonely — or undernourished — men had written proposing marriage.

She had started as a signature. In 1921, a Gold Medal flour recipe contest at Washburn-Crosby flooded the company with mail. A vice president who watched the advertising decided a woman should sign the replies; women, he thought, would wonder at a man mixed up in a recipe contest. The name on those letters was Betty Crocker.

By the mid-1920s she had a voice. On WCCO in Minneapolis, then on stations across the country and later on the NBC network, the Betty Crocker Cooking School of the Air walked listeners through recipes as if a friend had dropped by for the morning. Different women read the scripts in different cities. The letters still went out signed Betty. Listeners enrolled by the hundreds of thousands. Home economists answered the mail. The signature stayed soft and steady.

People asked her what to bake with ration stamps. They asked how to stretch a sack of flour. They asked for help the way you ask someone you trust. Some of the men who wrote wanted more than a cake. Ten of them, Fortune later counted, proposed.

When the magazine published its General Mills piece, the company was already more than flour — fats and oils, wartime side ventures, a brand built around a homemaker who answered the post. The outing did not empty the mailbox. The cake mix kept her face. Decades later the same name still sits on boxes and cookbooks, and registering on bettycrocker.com still feels a little like meeting someone.

In 1945 the photograph did the quiet work. A board chairman looked out from the page. Under him, the magazine said he was Betty. The women who had written her for dinner ideas, and the ten men who had asked for her hand, had already lived with the peer the caption unmade.
