Four-year-old Gus Lamont was last seen on a Saturday in late September 2025, playing on a mound of dirt at his family’s homestead south of Yunta, in South Australia’s outback. When his grandmother came back, he was gone.

Police, emergency volunteers and soldiers searched the property. Drones flew. Trackers walked. Assistant Commissioner Ian Parrott told the public there was no evidence of foul play, and that they believed the boy had wandered off. No footprint trail held. No hat. No Minions shirt in the dust.

A search that finds nothing is a bad post. Within days there were better ones.

On Facebook and Instagram, pages put up an image of a man loading a curly-haired blonde child into a four-wheel drive, under the words *is this a kidnapping case?* Some versions ran it as a breakthrough — an eyewitness sighting about a hundred kilometres from Yunta. Real photographs of Gus and of the search were cut into the same collage, so the invented part had something true to stand on.

The caption on that post asked people to stay alert and share the update, because a single tip can change everything, and said its hearts were with Gus’s family. People shared it by the thousands. Some of them believed he had been found.

Nobody running those pages was looking for Gus Lamont. His name had started to move, and a name that is moving is stock.

The posts got louder as they competed with each other. A miracle in the outback. A reunion with his loved ones, staged with police in American-looking uniforms. A soaked backpack. A hidden cave. Bloodstains. A body part. One Facebook AI search answer told users a toy had been found “with blood on it,” and that the boy was alive.

South Australia Police told [AFP Fact Check](https://factcheck.afp.com/doc.afp.com.78B22ZR) the carrying image was “not factual.” Look closely and the man’s fingers warp; a tree floats like a ghost. [AAP FactCheck](https://www.aap.com.au/factcheck/false-claims-about-missing-gus-lamont-spread-on-social-media/) traced engagement-bait pages managed from Vietnam. Nothing about the outback mattered to them. Yunta was where the traffic was that week, the way somewhere else had been the week before, and somewhere else would be the week after.

SA’s Commissioner for Victims’ Rights, Sarah Quick, said the fakes added an “extra layer of trauma” for a family already underwater, and had “very real consequences” for the people still looking. None of that appears anywhere in the arithmetic that produced the posts. There is no line in it for a grandmother.

The search on the ground scaled back. The search in the feed kept minting breakthroughs.

:::note{title="A cousin problem"}
A page that invents a kidnapping so the search looks settled is doing the same
job as [AI text crowding out the words people used to write for
themselves](https://anth.us/blog/jevons-paradox-ai-slop/) and as
[bots that pretend to be people in a discussion](https://chattic.us/thoughts/dont-pretend-bots-are-human/).
The copy turns up looking like the real thing, and it turns up because somebody
is paid by the turning up.
:::

Digging a shaft costs money. Flying a drone costs money. Walking the ground south of Yunta took police and volunteers and soldiers, and it turned up nothing. Making the picture cost nothing at all, and it was the only version of that week that paid.
