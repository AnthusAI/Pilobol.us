Imagine you turn it off.

For a week your phone only shows people you already follow, in the order they posted. No “For you.” No sudden stranger yelling into a camera. No unfinished argument that somehow knows your thumb. The glass goes quiet. It feels unfinished. Boring. Less like the world.

Most people will switch the ranking back on before the week is over. That is not a failure of willpower. That is the infection doing what it does: the preferred copy of social life is denser and smoother than the chronological room, and after years inside it, the territory looks wrong.

Australia is drafting laws that would force big platforms to offer a way out — pop-ups asking whether you want recommended content or only what you follow. Labor’s shape is mostly **opt-out**: the ranked room stays the default; you have to notice and leave. Consent advocate Chanel Contos told the National Press Club the government should seek **opt-in** instead — make the algorithm ask permission — arguing ranked feeds help fuel sexual violence and push young people toward misogynistic “manosphere” material. The Greens lean that way too. The Coalition calls parts of the fight censorship. The bill also talks about a digital duty of care and fines large enough to make a headline.

Contos is right that the pipe delivers harm at industrial scale. Ranking is not a neutral hose. It is a machine that builds a world *for you* — and that world can include the manosphere the way a flood includes whatever was upstream. Opt-in is more honest than opt-out. Opt-out asks the host to leave a room they already like. Opt-in stops treating the infection as the air.

But choice is not a cure for liking the simulacrum.

The chronological feed is the territory: messy, sparse, people you actually know, posting in time. The algorithm is the map that feels better than the territory — more intense, more coherent, more finished. People moved in because the copy was nicer. Amplification entrepreneurs (the farms that mint a kidnapping still while a boy is still missing; the old newspaper extras around a crib in Hopewell) need that denser room to sell plot. Violence-as-content needs it too. The fungus did not invent the itch for a finished story or the appetite for a smoother social life. It accelerated them until the unranked room feels like a draft.

So: opt-in over opt-out, if the question is honesty about consent. Do not confuse either with waking up. The darker fact is that after the pop-up, most thumbs will choose the ranked room again — because they like it.

That is the seam. The copy wins when leaving feels like leaving reality.
